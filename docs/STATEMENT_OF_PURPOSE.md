# Statement of Purpose

## Research Internship & PhD Applications in Robotics and Intelligent Systems

---

I still remember the first time I tried to make a robot understand what I wanted it to do. I told it to "pick up the red block," and it just sat there, completely still. Not because the hardware failed or the code crashed—but because I hadn't given it the ability to connect words to actions, to see the world and make sense of it, and to figure out how to move in a way that made sense. That moment stuck with me. It made me realize that building robots that can work alongside people isn't just about better motors or faster computers. It's about bridging the gap between how humans naturally communicate and how machines process information.

Over the past few years, I've been working on exactly that problem. I've built a system that lets robots understand natural language commands and execute manipulation tasks in simulation. It's not fancy for the sake of being fancy—it's designed to work reliably, to be understandable when things go wrong, and to be built in a way that lets me swap out components as better techniques emerge. I chose to make it modular rather than end-to-end because I wanted to actually understand what was happening at each step. When the robot fails to pick something up, I can trace whether it was a perception issue, a planning mistake, or a control problem. That kind of clarity matters when you're trying to make real progress.

The system combines computer vision for detecting objects, natural language parsing to interpret commands, symbolic planning to figure out action sequences, and custom inverse kinematics for control. I spent a lot of time getting the details right—handling singularities in the IK solver, making the perception robust to different lighting conditions, ensuring the planner could handle spatial relations like "left of" or "on top of." These aren't groundbreaking ideas individually, but putting them together into something that actually works taught me what it takes to do robotics research: you need to understand the fundamentals deeply, care about making things work reliably, and be willing to iterate when things don't go as planned.

What drives me is the vision of robots that can collaborate with humans in everyday settings. I'm not talking about robots replacing people—I'm interested in systems that extend what we can do, that can understand instructions given in plain language and adapt to new situations without needing to be reprogrammed. The current state of the field is exciting but also frustrating. We have powerful learning methods, but they often need massive amounts of data and fail in unpredictable ways. We have classical approaches that are interpretable and reliable, but they struggle with the complexity and variability of the real world. I think the most interesting research right now is at the intersection of these approaches—finding ways to bring learning and reasoning together.

I want to work on problems that matter. One area I find particularly compelling is how robots can learn from experience while maintaining the kind of structured reasoning that lets them generalize reliably. Can we build systems that learn perceptual representations from data but use symbolic planning to compose them in new ways? How do we make robots that can ask for help when they're uncertain, rather than failing silently or making dangerous guesses? How do we move beyond simulation to systems that can handle the messiness and variability of real physical environments?

I'm drawn to research environments where people care about building things that work, not just writing papers. I want to be around people who are excited about the technical details, who debug their robots at 2 AM because they're genuinely curious about why something isn't working, and who think carefully about what problems are worth solving. I'm looking for a place where rigor and creativity coexist—where mathematical foundations matter, but so does the intuition that comes from actually building and testing systems.

I know I have a lot to learn. I've mostly worked in simulation, and I want to understand how to bridge the gap to real hardware. I've focused on manipulation, but I'm curious about how these ideas extend to mobile robots, to multi-robot systems, to longer-horizon tasks that require persistent world models. I want to learn from people who have spent years thinking about these problems, who have the experience to know which directions are promising and which are dead ends.

What I can bring is a combination of technical skill, genuine curiosity, and a willingness to work hard on difficult problems. I've taught myself how to implement inverse kinematics from scratch, how to debug perception systems that fail in subtle ways, how to design experiments that actually test what you think they're testing. I'm comfortable with math, I can code well enough to build systems that others can actually use, and I've learned how to read papers critically—not just accepting what they say, but thinking about what assumptions they make and what they're leaving out.

More than specific technical skills, though, I bring an approach to research. I care about understanding things deeply rather than just getting them to work. I like building systems that are clear and maintainable, because I think that's how you make real progress rather than just piling hacks on top of hacks. I'm comfortable with failure—most of what I've tried hasn't worked, and that's taught me to be systematic about debugging and thoughtful about experimental design.
