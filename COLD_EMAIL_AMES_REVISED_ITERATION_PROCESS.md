During my R&D internship at Ansys, I developed control algorithms and digital twin simulations for robotic systems. The most relevant aspects to your work:

- **Model-based control design:** I implemented trajectory optimization for robotic manipulators, which exposed me to the challenges of contact-rich manipulation and the need for real-time constraint satisfaction
- **Safety verification:** I worked on workspace analysis for collision avoidance, where I saw firsthand how traditional reachability methods fail to scale to high-dimensional systems with learning-based components
- **Simulation infrastructure:** I built digital twin environments for testing controllers before hardware deployment, which taught me the importance of sim-to-real transfer and model accuracy

These experiences revealed a critical gap: while learning-based methods excel at capturing complex dynamics, integrating them with formal safety guarantees remains challenging.

**Research That Resonates:**

Your CLF-CBF-QP framework elegantly addresses this gap. Reading "Control Barrier Functions: Theory and Applications," I was struck by how barrier functions provide a mathematically principled way to enforce safety constraints in real-time optimal control. The key insight—that safety can be maintained by ensuring the barrier function's derivative satisfies a simple inequality—enables composition with learning-based approaches.

Your more recent work on "Learning Certified Control Using Contraction Metrics" and papers on safety filters for learning-enabled controllers show how this framework extends to learned dynamics. What particularly interests me is how you handle the hybrid nature of legged locomotion. In "Composition of Control Barrier Functions for Hybrid Systems," you address the challenge of maintaining safety across discrete contact transitions—this is crucial for bipedal walking where contact switches fundamentally change system dynamics.

**Robotics Projects and Relevance:**

In my multi-robot coordination work, I implemented MPC-based collision avoidance for autonomous navigation. While this worked in simulation, I faced two key challenges:
1. **Real-time computation:** MPC struggled to solve optimization problems fast enough as the number of robots scaled
2. **Safety under uncertainty:** Uncertain robot dynamics made it difficult to guarantee collision avoidance

Your CLF-CBF-QP approach could address both: barrier functions provide real-time safety guarantees through efficient QP solving, and the framework naturally handles bounded uncertainty. I'm curious whether similar principles could apply to multi-contact scenarios in legged locomotion, where each contact point introduces constraints analogous to multi-agent collision avoidance.

**Concrete Contributions:**

I see several ways I could contribute to your lab's research:

1. **Learning contact models:** My publications on state-space models and deep learning could support learning contact dynamics for legged robots. Specifically, I'm interested in how recurrent architectures might capture the temporal dependencies in contact sequences during walking.

2. **Sim-to-real validation:** My experience building digital twins at Ansys could help develop simulation environments for testing learned locomotion controllers. This could accelerate the development cycle and provide safe environments for initial policy learning.

3. **Multi-contact coordination:** My work on multi-agent systems might offer insights for coordinating multiple contact points in whole-body control. The parallels between distributed multi-robot coordination and multi-contact force distribution intrigue me.

**Research Questions I'm Exploring:**

- How can we learn barrier functions jointly with locomotion policies to achieve tighter safety margins and more agile behaviors?
- Can contraction metrics learned from demonstrations provide stronger stability guarantees than fixed Lyapunov functions?
- How do we handle the combinatorial complexity of contact sequences in learning-based planners while maintaining safety?

I'm applying through Caltech SURF and would be thrilled to contribute to the AMBER Lab's work on making legged robots both safe and agile. I'm particularly excited about ongoing projects in learning-based control for dynamic locomotion.
